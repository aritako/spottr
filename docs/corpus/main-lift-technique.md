# Main Lift Technique Fundamentals

The main barbell lifts, squat, hinge, press, and pull, are skills. Like any skill, they reward a consistent setup, a repeatable path, and practice that stays within the technique's quality limits. Good technique is not aesthetic; it is how load reaches the target muscles safely, and it is the difference between a number that counts and a number that flirting with injury produced.

A few principles apply across all the main lifts. Brace before every heavy rep: take a big breath into the belly, tighten the trunk as if bracing for a punch, and hold it through the rep. Maintain a neutral spine under load; the lumbar spine is built to handle compressive force well and shear poorly, and flexing or rounding under a heavy bar shifts loading toward the structures least equipped for it. Keep the bar close and the path efficient: in the squat, press, and deadlift the bar wants to travel near vertical over the midfoot, and drift is both harder and riskier. Control the lowering phase and drive the lifting phase, rather than dropping and catching. And make every setup identical: same stance, same grip, same sequence every rep, because technique that varies rep to rep never becomes automatic, and variable technique undermines every progression decision built on top of it.

For the squat, stance and bar position are individual. Anthropometry, the relative lengths of the femur, tibia, and torso, determines whether a wider or narrower stance and a high-bar or low-bar position let a lifter hit depth with a neutral spine; a low-bar position sits the bar lower on the back and trades some knee demand for more hip hinge and forward torso lean, while high-bar keeps the torso more upright. Depth is best defined functionally: as deep as mobility allows while the spine stays neutral, which for most lifters means below parallel. Deep squats are not inherently dangerous for healthy knees; a systematic review of squat depth and joint loading found the injury concerns about deep squats unfounded for tissues conditioned to training, and it noted that half and quarter squats, which allow much heavier supra-maximal loading through a short range, may actually be the worse long-term bet for joint health. Let the knees travel naturally over the toes; forcing them strictly back shifts the demand to the hips and can compromise depth.

For the hinge and deadlift, the bar stays against the body throughout: set up with the bar over the midfoot, take the slack out, wedge the hips back, keep the back flat, and push the floor away rather than pulling with the arms. Lock out by standing tall with the glutes, not by hyperextending the lower back. For pressing, set the shoulder blades, control the bar's descent along an efficient path, and press from a stable base. For pulls, initiate by depressing the shoulder blades and driving the elbows, and get the chest to the bar without turning it into a swing.

Fatigue is the enemy of technique, and technique is the guardrail against injury. Review work on squat biomechanics notes that fatigue degrades the pattern, increasing knee instability and lumbar shear risk, which is a strong argument for ending heavy work when form breaks down rather than when the set count says so. This is one reason working sets on the main lifts are best kept at one to three reps in reserve: the last reps of a true max effort set are where technique unravels. Warming up with ramping sets grooves the pattern before load arrives, and filming top sets occasionally is the cheapest coaching available.

Spottr's per-exercise trends assume technique consistency: an estimated one-rep max that rises because depth got shallower is not progress. When the numbers say one thing and the videos say another, believe the videos.
