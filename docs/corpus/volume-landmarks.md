# Volume Landmarks for Hypertrophy

Training volume, counted as hard working sets per muscle group per week, is the most influential programming variable for muscle growth. The volume landmarks framework popularized by Mike Israetel and Renaissance Periodization gives lifters a vocabulary for reasoning about how much volume is enough, how much is optimal, and how much is too much. A hard set means a set taken within roughly zero to three reps of failure. Easy, distant-from-failure sets do not count toward the landmarks.

There are four volume landmarks. Maintenance volume, MV, is the lowest number of weekly sets that keeps the muscle you already have. Minimum effective volume, MEV, is the lowest number of weekly sets that produces new growth. Maximum adaptive volume, MAV, is the range of weekly sets where the best gains happen for most of a training block. Maximum recoverable volume, MRV, is the ceiling beyond which added sets stop helping and start accumulating fatigue faster than the body can absorb. Productive training lives between MEV and MRV.

As rough starting points for intermediate lifters, common coaching estimates put maintenance volume near 4 to 8 weekly sets per muscle, minimum effective volume near 6 to 10, the adaptive range somewhere around 12 to 20, and maximum recoverable volume often in the low twenties, with large differences between muscles and individuals. Treat these numbers as starting points to calibrate against your own training log, not as laws. The research direction is clear even where exact values are not: a meta-analysis of weekly set volume found a graded dose-response, with ten or more weekly sets per muscle outgrowing lower volumes, and each additional weekly set contributing a small further gain. Diminishing returns and rising fatigue eventually cap the benefit of more volume.

Volume landmarks are used across a training block, or mesocycle, rather than as fixed weekly targets. A standard block starts near minimum effective volume, adds sets each week as the muscle adapts and tolerance builds, approaches maximum recoverable volume in the final hard week, then deloads. After the deload, the next block usually restarts slightly ahead of where the previous one began, because adapted muscle generally needs a bit more volume to keep growing. That slow escalation across blocks is one of the most practical forms of progressive overload available to intermediate lifters.

You find your own landmarks by reading your response. If a muscle is not growing on your current weekly sets, you are probably below its minimum effective volume. If performance on a muscle's lifts is dropping week over week, joints are aching, or the muscle feels run down before sessions even start, you are likely above its maximum recoverable volume and a deload is due. Between those signals, more volume generally means more growth, so long as recovery holds.

Spottr helps make volume visible. Weekly tonnage per exercise, which Spottr computes from every logged set, tracks how much work each muscle is actually receiving, and a sudden multi-week drop in tonnage while programming is unchanged can be an early sign that fatigue is accumulating. Tonnage is a proxy rather than a set count, so use it as a trend signal and cross-check it against how many hard sets you remember doing per muscle each week.

Volume is not the only variable that matters, and more is not automatically better. Effort per set, exercise selection, and recovery capacity all shape the response. But if a lifter asks where to start when growth has stalled, the volume question, am I between my minimum effective volume and my maximum recoverable volume for this muscle, is usually the right first question.
