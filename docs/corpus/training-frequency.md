# Training Frequency

Training frequency has two meanings that get mixed together. Per-muscle frequency is how many times per week a given muscle is trained directly. Session frequency is how many workouts per week a lifter performs. Most coaching debates are about the first: should a muscle be hit once, twice, or three times per week.

The early evidence looked favorable to high frequency. A 2016 meta-analysis found that training a muscle two to three days per week produced more hypertrophy than training it once per week. But later work refined the picture. When weekly volume is equated, meaning the same number of hard sets per muscle per week spread across more or fewer sessions, the frequency advantage largely disappears: a 2019 volume-equated meta-analysis found the difference between training a muscle once versus multiple times per week trivial and not statistically meaningful. For strength, a separate meta-analysis of 22 studies found higher frequency associated with greater strength gains, but the effect was primarily driven by extra volume; once volume was equated, frequency alone had no significant effect.

The correct conclusion is not that frequency is useless. It is that within the range of one to three direct sessions per muscle per week, frequency mainly works as a volume distribution tool. Total weekly hard sets and per-set effort drive growth; frequency determines how those sets are spread, which affects their quality.

Distributing volume across two or three weekly sessions per muscle has real advantages. First, session quality: ten hard sets of pulling in one session means the last three or four sets are usually junk volume performed in a fatigued state, while the same ten sets split across two sessions stay productive. Second, the repeated stimulus spreads muscle protein synthesis across the week rather than one big spike. Third, skill: main lifts like squat, bench press, and deadlift are skills, and practicing a skill more frequently, even at moderate loads, improves the coordination that heavy lifting demands.

Once-a-week body-part split training still works, and plenty of lifters have grown on it, but it makes high volumes harder to perform well and leaves each muscle untrained for six days between sessions. A practical default for most lifters is to train each major muscle group two or preferably three times per week, choosing a weekly split, such as upper and lower days or full-body sessions, that makes that distribution natural without doubling total volume beyond recoverable limits.

There is no evidence that extreme frequencies, training the same muscle hard every single day, outperform sensible distribution, and recovery limits bind quickly. The sweet spot is the frequency that lets you complete your planned weekly volume with good technique and genuine proximity to failure on every hard set, which for most people lands around two to three sessions per muscle per week.

Spottr makes frequency visible in the log. Weekly tonnage per exercise shows how a muscle's volume is actually distributed across the week, and a per-muscle pattern that concentrates enormous tonnage into a single day is often a sign that redistribution across two or three days would let the same weekly volume be performed better.
