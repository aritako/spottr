# Autoregulation with RIR and RPE

Autoregulation means adjusting training to the lifter's actual capacity on the day, rather than following fixed prescriptions that assume capacity never changes. Real strength fluctuates day to day with sleep, stress, nutrition, and accumulated fatigue, often by several percent. A program that ignores those fluctuations either leaves performance on the table on good days or buries the lifter on bad ones.

The most accessible autoregulation tools are RIR and RPE. RIR stands for repetitions in reserve: how many more clean reps you could have done at the end of a set. RPE, the rating of perceived exertion, is scored from 1 to 10 and, in the resistance-training-specific scale developed by Zourdos and colleagues, maps directly onto RIR near the top: RPE 10 means zero reps in reserve, muscular failure; RPE 9 means about one rep left; RPE 8 means about two reps left; RPE 7 means about three. Below that the scale describes general effort. So a prescription of three sets of five at RPE 8 means: choose a weight you could lift for roughly seven, and do five.

Prescribing by RIR or RPE adjusts load automatically. Instead of a fixed percentage of one-rep max, which bakes in an assumption about today's strength, the lifter selects whatever load achieves the target effort. On a strong day that load is heavier; on a tired, under-slept day it is lighter, and the stimulus stays appropriate either way. Research and reviews on autoregulation describe this as aligning the training stimulus with daily readiness.

Accuracy is the main limitation. Experienced lifters can typically rate their sets within about one rep of the true RIR. Novices are systematically poor at it: they report being near failure when three or more repetitions remain, because nothing in their training history has calibrated the sensation. Calibration comes from occasional true failure sets, most safely on isolation exercises and machine lifts, where the cost of misjudging is low. Novices should also bias toward double progression, letting rep targets decide load increases, and treat their RIR ratings as a skill under construction.

A second use is autoregulating volume. One approach is the RPE stop: perform back-off sets at a fixed load until the set's RPE rises a set number above the first set, then end the exercise. This lets a fresh session naturally hold more volume and a fatigued session naturally hold less, without changing the plan on paper.

Practical defaults for effort: most working sets should land at RPE 7 to 9, roughly one to three reps in reserve. Heavy top sets on the main lifts are best kept at RPE 8 to 9 rather than 10, because grinding true max-effort singles and triples repeatedly accumulates fatigue and technique breakdown. True failure is a spice, not a staple: an occasional final set of an isolation lift taken to RPE 10 teaches calibration and can add stimulus, but whole sessions of failure training generally cost more in fatigue than they return in growth.

Autoregulation does not replace progressive overload or periodization; it smooths their execution. The plan still says what to progress and when to deload; RIR and RPE decide how heavy today's work actually is. A useful discipline is to log RPE alongside each set, because a week where the same loads all felt two RIR harder than usual is exactly the early warning a plan responds to.
