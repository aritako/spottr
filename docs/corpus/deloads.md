# Deloads

A deload is a planned period, usually one week, of sharply reduced training volume that lets accumulated fatigue dissipate while the habit of training and the skill of the lifts stay intact. It is not a rest week and it is not a vacation from the gym; it is a lower-volume week with the same movements, chosen on purpose, at the right time.

The logic comes from the fitness-fatigue relationship. Hard training raises fitness and fatigue together. Fitness decays slowly over weeks, while fatigue decays over days. When fatigue piles up, it masks the fitness you have already built, which is why a beat-up lifter can be stronger than their recent performances show. Cutting volume hard for a short window drains the fatigue while barely touching the fitness, so performance rebounds, often above where it stalled. Tapering research in sport shows the pattern clearly: reducing training volume dramatically while keeping some intensity, over roughly one to two weeks, reliably improves performance.

The structure of a deload week is simple. Cut the number of working sets roughly in half, keeping roughly the same exercises, the same frequency, and loads in a moderate range rather than maximal. Drop most sets well away from failure, around three to five reps in reserve, saving the body from grinding while still greasing technique and moving real weight. A deload week is not the place for rep-max testing or new exercise maxima. Recent experimental work supports the concept: a randomized trial of one-week deloads within supervised resistance training found the deload weeks did not compromise the muscular adaptations gained across the program.

Timing can be planned or reactive. Planned: end every mesocycle with a deload, most naturally in the week after volumes have climbed to their highest point near maximum recoverable volume, which for most intermediate lifters means every four to eight weeks. Reactive: take a deload early when the warning signs appear, including performance dropping across consecutive sessions on unchanged weights, persistent joint aches, disturbed sleep or appetite, and dread of training that goes beyond ordinary laziness. One bad session means nothing; a week of bad sessions and sore joints means the fatigue bill has arrived.

Coming out of the deload, do not jump straight back to peak volumes. Restart at around two-thirds of the pre-deload working sets and climb again over the following weeks, letting progressive overload rebuild the stimulus. Each new mesocycle typically starts slightly ahead of where the previous one started, and well below where it ended.

Common mistakes deserve naming. Some lifters skip deloads entirely because they feel fine, which works until it abruptly does not, and the forced layoff from injury or illness costs far more than the planned week ever would. Others rest completely for the deload week, which surrenders the skill practice and the active-recovery benefit of light training. Others turn the deload into a test week, chasing fresh personal bests because they feel recovered, which re-adds fatigue precisely when the goal was to remove it.

Spottr makes the deload visible in retrospect. Weekly tonnage per exercise should drop noticeably during the deload week, and estimated one-rep max values may read slightly lower because the week contains fewer near-maximal sets, which is expected and not a decline. The week after, tonnage should resume its climb from the reduced base.
